# The 22,000% Question

(See civic-engagement-roi.html for the current editable version with graphs and references. This markdown file is outdated.)

Elon Musk spent $288 million to help elect Donald Trump in 2024. Tesla stock jumped 75% after the election. His net worth increased by over $200 billion. On a $288 million investment, that's a return of about 70,000%.

This is the number that makes people cynical about democracy, and it should. It's a staggering return. But it's worth asking: return for whom?

The answer is obvious. The return goes to the company. The policy outcome -- in this case, a tax holiday for repatriating offshore profits -- benefits shareholders. The public absorbed the cost. That's the whole game. You spend money to get a policy that makes you more money, and everyone else pays for it.

But there's a different way companies can spend money on politics, and it has its own ROI story.

## The other approach

Patagonia gives its employees Election Day off. It co-founded Time to Vote in 2018 with Levi Strauss and PayPal -- a coalition that grew from 411 companies to over 2,000, all pledging paid time off or flexible scheduling so employees can vote. In 2020, Patagonia sewed "Vote the Assholes Out" into clothing tags. One designer's idea. Near-zero marginal cost. Enormous earned media.

This isn't lobbying. Nobody is buying a specific policy outcome. The investment is in democratic participation itself -- more people voting, period.

So what does this cost, and what does it produce?

## The math

Decades of political science research (primarily Green and Gerber at Yale and Columbia) have nailed down the cost of generating one additional vote through various methods:

- Door-to-door canvassing: $19-31 per vote
- Volunteer phone calls: $20-35 per vote
- Direct mail: $91-137 per vote
- TV advertising (the thing political donations mostly fund): $200-365 per vote

Corporate voter mobilization -- giving employees time off, running registration drives, sending reminders -- probably falls at or below the canvassing range. The company already has a direct relationship with the person. No doors to knock on. No strangers to convince. Just "hey, go vote, here's the time to do it."

One day's wages for an employee earning $60,000 is about $230. If even a fraction of those employees vote who otherwise wouldn't have, the cost per additional vote is competitive with the cheapest known method. And unlike canvassing, it scales without hiring anyone.

## Gross vs. less gross

Here's where it gets interesting. Lobbying's 22,000% ROI is private. The return accrues to the company. The policy it buys is often zero-sum or worse for the public. And people know it -- corporate political spending consistently ranks as one of the most distrusted forms of institutional behavior.

Civic engagement spending is different in kind. The "return" is distributed. More people vote. Elections become marginally more representative. No specific policy is purchased. And the brand effect is positive: 79% of consumers say companies should take civic action, and nonpartisan civic engagement polls well across party lines. Partisan donations, by contrast, have a roughly symmetric attract/repel effect on consumers -- for every customer you gain, you lose one.

So the comparison isn't really about ROI in the same sense. It's about what kind of power you're buying. Lobbying buys a specific policy outcome for yourself. Civic engagement buys a slightly more functional democracy for everyone. The first one is a better investment if you're a shareholder. The second one is a better investment if you're a citizen.

## The worker-owned angle

This is where it connects to something deeper. A worker-owned company has a different incentive structure. The shareholders ARE the workers. The workers ARE the citizens. The gap between "good for the company" and "good for the public" is narrower by design.

When a conventional corporation spends on lobbying, it's doing exactly what its structure incentivizes: maximizing returns for shareholders who are not the workers, not the customers, not the community. When a worker-owned company invests in civic participation, it's doing the same thing -- acting in the interest of its owners. It just happens that its owners are regular people who benefit from a functioning democracy.

This doesn't make worker-owned companies saints. But it does mean the 22,000% question hits differently. A company owned by its workers is less likely to spend on lobbying that screws the public because the public IS the ownership. The incentives are aligned in a way that conventional corporate structure can never fully replicate, no matter how many ESG reports it publishes.

## So what?

The point isn't that civic engagement is a better investment than lobbying. By the numbers, it obviously isn't -- not for the company. The point is that the 22,000% ROI of lobbying is a symptom of a broken system, and the fact that we celebrate it as clever rather than condemn it as corrupt tells you something about how we think about corporations.

Companies like Patagonia are trying a different thing. It costs less. It helps more people. And it doesn't require buying a politician. That's not a 22,000% return. But it might be a return worth having.
